Most finance teams I meet spend the bulk of each month **assembling** numbers and only a sliver **explaining** them. Exports from the ERP, pasting into the MIS template, re-linking the pivot that broke, chasing the one entity that posted late — and then a rushed afternoon writing variance commentary before the board pack goes out.

AI changes that ratio. But only if you redesign the work, not just bolt a chatbot onto the old process.

## 1. Automate the assembly, not the judgement

The first win is unglamorous: let software rebuild the pack from the raw ledger every time. Trial balance in, statements, schedules and board slides out — the same way every month, with no hand-keyed numbers. When the build is a repeatable script rather than a spreadsheet ritual, a late posting stops being a crisis. You simply run it again.

Judgement stays with people. The machine assembles; the finance team decides what the numbers mean.

## 2. Put controls inside the pipeline

Automation without controls just produces wrong numbers faster. Every automated run should prove itself:

- Balance sheet balances, and the P&L ties to retained earnings.
- Revenue and cost ties back to the ledger, entity by entity, to nil.
- Prior-month figures are unchanged unless someone meant to change them.
- Anything new — a new customer, a new GL account — is flagged for a human before it is mapped.

If a control fails, the pack is labelled **draft** and the reason is printed. Reviewers stop re-performing the build and start reviewing exceptions.

## 3. Let AI draft the first version of the story

Large language models are good at turning a variance table into a first draft of commentary: what moved, by how much, and which line items drove it. They are not good at knowing *why* — that the price increase was a one-off, or that a plant was down for maintenance. So the right pattern is: the model drafts, the business owner corrects, and the corrected comment becomes part of the record.

## 4. Measure the close in hours, not days

Once assembly and tie-outs are automated, the critical path of the close becomes the slowest human decision, not the slowest spreadsheet. That is a much better problem to have — and a much more visible one.

## Where to start

Pick one monthly report that everyone complains about. Script the build end to end, add five hard controls, and run it in parallel with the manual version for two months. When the two agree, switch off the manual one. Then do the next report.

That is what an AI CFO does in practice: not replacing the finance team, but giving it back the time to be the intelligence function the business needs.
